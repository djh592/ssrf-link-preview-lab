Here are the results. In the first two rows, the number shows how often the internal report was reached, so zero is the safe result. Each row combines two scenarios, with 50 trials for each scenario and policy. [Click] For direct internal targets, No Check reached the report in all 100 trials. Every policy that checked the initial destination blocked all 100 attempts. [Click] The result changed when we introduced redirects. Initial URL blocked direct access, but it failed in all 100 redirect trials because it did not check the new destination. Each Hop and Check and Bind blocked every redirect attack. No Redirects also blocked them by refusing to follow the redirect. Across all 1,000 repeated attack trials, report access and marker disclosure gave the same result. [Click] For DNS rebinding, we compare two individual probes under the same TTL-zero and empty-cache condition. Each Hop approved an external address but connected to the internal report. Check and Bind stayed on the approved external peer. These are individual mechanism probes, not attack rates. [Click] For normal tasks, the first four policies succeeded in all 150 trials. No Redirects succeeded in only 50, because it rejected the 100 legitimate previews that required redirects. Check and Bind added a small measured cost. The median direct-page latency increased from 5.7 to 6.9 milliseconds, and P95 increased from 57.6 to 62.5 milliseconds. [Click] This is why we focus on Check and Bind. It blocked the repeated direct and redirect attacks, kept the external peer in the matched DNS probe, and preserved all tested normal previews. [Click] Within our tested conditions, Check and Bind closed both observed destination-change gaps without sacrificing tested preview behavior.
